Cognition, Culture & Well-Being

Analytic-Holistic Thinking and Cultural Fit: City Environment, Congruence, and Subjective Well-Being

A multi-method investigation of whether congruence between an individual’s thinking style and their proximal and city-level cultural environments is associated with greater subjective well-being across Chinese cities.

#Cultural Fit#Analytic-Holistic Thinking #Subjective Well-Being#Chinese Cities

Research Focus

Cultural fit across proximal and city environments

This study examines whether the degree of congruence between an individual’s analytic-holistic thinking style and their cultural environment—measured at both the perceived proximal level (peers and surrounding environment) and the aggregated distal level (city-level thinking styles and cultural tightness)—is associated with higher subjective well-being (SWB) in Chinese cities.

Primary Purpose
Test multi-level cultural fit

Directly test person-environment cultural fit across individual, proximal, and city-level cognitive metrics.

Secondary Purpose
Reveal underlying mechanisms

Explore behavioral decisions, cognitive conflict, physiological mechanisms, and moderators such as self-efficacy.

Progressive Multi-Method Design

Research Design

Three connected studies move from ecological associations to real-time cognitive processing and incentivized decision-making.

01

Ecological Level

Cross-Sectional Survey

Establishes macro- and meso-level evidence linking cognitive-environmental fit with well-being across a large sample of Chinese cities.

Measures
Analysis-Holism Scale (AHS), perceived proximal AHS, WHO-5 Well-Being Index, and Cultural Tightness Scale
Analysis
Response Surface Analysis (RSA) and Multilevel Modeling (MLM)
Current refinement Two AHS items may not be fully suitable for Chinese participants. Open-ended questions are therefore being incorporated to supplement the scale through text analysis.
02

Cognitive Process Level

Online Mouse-Tracking Experiment

Provides behavioral validation of cognitive processing and decision conflict during dialectical judgments, complementing the self-report AHS measure.

Technique
Real-time online mouse tracking during the processing of contradictory statements
Metrics
Trajectory curvature, maximum deviation, and response time
03

Incentivized Behavior & Physiological Level

Real-Incentive Allocation Task & Neuroimaging

Delivers construct validity and neurophysiological convergence for Studies 1 and 2 through a high-ecological-validity decision task conducted across multiple universities.

Allocation Targets
Self, local city, past residential cities, unfamiliar cities varying in cultural tightness, and national charities
Physiology
Planned fNIRS/EEG measures of value-related activation, including vmPFC/striatum activity and LPP amplitude

Expected Evidence

Expected Research Outcomes

H1
Macro/Meso Fit Effect

Greater alignment among individual AHS, perceived proximal AHS, and city-level aggregate AHS or tightness will predict higher WHO-5 well-being.

H2
Behavioral Trajectory Patterns

More holistic thinkers—especially on attitude toward contradiction—will show greater mouse-trajectory curvature and deviation during conflict.

H3a / H3a′
Allocation & Context Integration

Higher fit will predict more contextual, balanced allocation. Unfamiliar-city tightness will shape allocation amounts depending on individual AHS.

H3b / H3c · Exploratory
Physiology & Self-Efficacy

03 · Challenge CupPsychology

Initiative Research Project

Predictive Effect of Self-Objectification on Social Appearance Anxiety and Its Intervention Efficacy: The Moderating Roles of Attachment Style and Gender

A two-study empirical project examining how self-objectification predicts Social Appearance Anxiety, how attachment style and gender shape this pathway, and whether structured writing interventions can produce tailored psychological benefits.

Co-authored by Sally Hsiao & Kalam Kuan

#Self-Objectification#Social Appearance Anxiety#Attachment#Gender

Abstract

Objectification, Attachment, Gender & Intervention

Objectification Theory posits that self-objectification is a cognitive propensity wherein individuals adopt an observer’s perspective to evaluate their own bodies, disproportionately emphasizing physical appearance over internal needs. Prolonged exposure to this state triggers persistent body surveillance and body shame, ultimately leading to negative psychological outcomes such as appearance anxiety. Social Appearance Anxiety (SAA)—the distress arising from fear of negative evaluation regarding one’s overall physical appearance—has shown an upward trajectory in contemporary society, exerting a particularly pronounced impact on young adults.

Although empirical literature confirms that self-objectification positively predicts SAA, this research further investigates the moderating mechanisms of attachment style and gender through a two-part empirical design. Study 1 examines the predictive pathway from self-objectification to SAA and tests moderation by attachment style and gender. Study 2 evaluates an intervention designed to reduce self-objectification and alleviate SAA, while testing whether those same characteristics shape its outcomes.

Study 1 found that self-objectification significantly and positively predicted SAA. Moderate-to-high attachment avoidance strengthened this relationship, while attachment anxiety showed a stable direct association with SAA but no significant moderation. The predictive link was marginally stronger among men. Study 2 found that body-functionality writing did not reduce self-objectification overall, whereas creative writing facilitated self-distancing and reduced self-objectification without substantively lowering SAA. Crucially, body-functionality writing attenuated SAA among participants high in attachment avoidance, and effects on self-objectification were limited to women.

Together, these findings clarify the distinct moderating roles of attachment and gender, refine Objectification Theory through a gender-sensitive lens, enrich Attachment Theory, and offer empirical guidance for tailoring individual-level SAA interventions.

01

Conceptual foundation

Background & Theoretical Framework

Social appearance anxiety (SAA) is the fear of being negatively evaluated because of one’s overall appearance, including body shape. (Hart et al., 2008).

Four broad domains of influence · Putri et al., 2025

Individual
Social-media and smartphone use, avoidance, coping, and social anxiety
Cognitive
Self-esteem, social comparison, ideal internalization, and body surveillance
Emotional
Insecure attachment, body shame, rejection sensitivity, and self-compassion
Social
Teasing and victimization, parental support, and culture
Objectification Theory

Fredrickson and Roberts (1997) propose that individuals—especially women—may internalize an observer’s perspective and treat their bodies as objects to be evaluated.

Attachment Theory

Bowlby (1969) and Brennan et al. (1998) frame attachment anxiety as fear of rejection and attachment avoidance as discomfort with intimacy, both of which shape responses to social evaluation.

Research Gap

Less is known about how interpersonal vulnerability changes the self-objectification–SAA pathway, or whether cognitive and expressive writing interventions work differently across attachment and gender profiles.

02

Six hypotheses

Hypotheses

Study 1 · Predictive Model

  1. H1

    Self-objectification positively predicts Social Appearance Anxiety.

  2. H2

    Attachment anxiety and avoidance amplify this positive relationship.

  3. H3

    The predictive association is stronger among women than men.

Study 2 · Intervention Model

  1. H4

    Body-functionality writing decreases self-objectification and subsequently lowers SAA.

  2. H5

    Attachment style moderates intervention efficacy.

  3. H6

    Women show greater intervention benefits than men.

03

Two-part empirical design

Experimental Design & Methodology

Study 1
Cross-Sectional Predictive Study

College students completed validated measures of Social Appearance Anxiety, self-objectification, body surveillance, and adult attachment.

Measures used Social Appearance Anxiety Scale (SAAS), Experiences in Close Relationships (ECR), Relationship Questionnaire (RQ), Self-Objectification Questionnaire (SOQ), OBCS – Body Surveillance, Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (SES), and Self-Compassion Scale (SCS).

Study 2
Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Trial

The intervention adapted Alleva et al.’s (2015) Expand Your Horizon paradigm. Participants completed three writing sessions focused on body functionality; the control group completed creativity-based short stories on the same schedule.

04

Empirical evidence

Key Findings & Results

Study 1

Predictive Pathway
01
Direct Predictive Effect

Self-objectification significantly predicted higher SAA, supporting H1.

02
Attachment Avoidance

Johnson–Neyman analysis showed that self-objectification predicted SAA when avoidance was moderate-to-high (≥ 59.48), covering 76% of the sample. Attachment anxiety remained a stable direct predictor but did not moderate the link.

03
Counter-Intuitive Gender Pattern

Contrary to H3, the association was significant in men (β = 9.03, p = .004) but not statistically significant in women (β = 1.51, p = .652).

Study 2

Intervention Efficacy
04
Self-Objectification Outcomes

Although self-objectification changed over time, the magnitude of this change did not differ significantly between the experimental and control groups. The intervention also did not moderate the temporal trajectory of self-objectification’s effect on SAA.

Within the experimental group, self-objectification significantly or marginally predicted SAA at t1, t2, and t4. Except at t3, higher self-objectification was associated with higher SAA. In the control group, this prediction reached marginal significance only at t3.

05
Distinct Attachment Effects

Notably, distinct attachment dimensions play differential roles in shaping social appearance anxiety during the intervention process:

  • The attachment anxiety dimension significantly predicts social appearance anxiety, such that higher attachment anxiety scores correspond to higher social appearance anxiety scores;
  • Attachment avoidance, on the other hand, functions as a key moderator of intervention efficacy, with the intervention demonstrating a significantly greater reduction in social appearance anxiety for individuals high in attachment avoidance relative to the control group.
06
Gender-Specific Effect

Gender moderated the intervention’s effect on self-objectification: the imagination-writing control task produced a significant “de-objectifying effect” among female participants.

However, gender did not moderate the intervention pathway for SAA. Reduced self-objectification therefore did not translate into lower SAA among women.

05

Interpretation & application

Discussion & Practical Insights

Study 1

Prediction, Attachment & Gender
Self-objectification predicts SAA

Self-objectification significantly predicted social appearance anxiety.

Attachment anxiety was a direct predictor, not a moderator

According to Attachment Theory (Bowlby, 1969), highly anxious individuals fear abandonment and over-attend to others’ evaluations. Their appearance anxiety may therefore arise directly from a fear of negative evaluation rather than from an interaction with self-objectification.

Anxious attachment centers on others’ global evaluation of the self, whereas Objectification Theory (Fredrickson & Roberts, 1997) centers on one’s own objectifying evaluation of the body. Their different mechanisms help explain the non-significant interaction.

Attachment avoidance was regionally significant

The moderating role of attachment style was level- and context-dependent rather than uniform across the full range of avoidance.

A male vulnerability pattern emerged

Self-objectification did not significantly predict SAA among women, but higher self-objectification predicted greater SAA among men. Women may develop a desensitization effect under chronic appearance evaluation, while less frequent objectification makes each episode more salient for men.

Gender Role Theory (Eagly, 1987) further suggests that expectations of toughness, independence, and competitiveness may intensify men’s motivation to meet social appearance standards and amplify anxiety (Ruan et al., 2025).

Study 2

Intervention Mechanisms
Body-functionality writing fell short of expectations

Unlike Alleva et al. (2015), body-functionality writing did not effectively reduce self-objectification. Creative writing did reduce it, although the change did not translate into lower SAA.

Writing directly about the body may still activate body surveillance. Creative writing instead invites imagined characters, worlds, and narratives, potentially creating self-distancing that dampens body-monitoring awareness.

State change did not alter the broader SAA trajectory

The intervention did not moderate the temporal pathway from self-objectification to SAA, although it changed when self-objectification predicted anxiety across the two groups. Self-objectification significantly and positively predicted SAA in the experimental group, whereas no comparable predictive pathway emerged in the control group.

Interpretation The fluctuations induced by writing tasks resemble state self-objectification (Moradi & Huang, 2008). Their limited effect on SAA indirectly suggests that trait self-objectification is the more consequential driver.
Attachment dimensions played distinct roles

Attachment anxiety remained a stable predictor of SAA, while attachment avoidance determined who benefited from the intervention. Body-functionality writing was especially effective for people high in avoidance.

Interpretation A self-administered, de-socialized writing task bypasses avoidant individuals’ interpersonal disclosure concerns and offers a low-threat route for alleviating SAA.
Gender affected self-objectification, not SAA

Female participants experienced a de-self-objectifying effect after creative writing, but this did not produce a corresponding reduction in social appearance anxiety.

04 · Award-Winning ProjectArt Therapy

Award-Winning General Education Project: Interactive Design for Art Therapy

A proposal for the Design of a Participatory Art Therapy Program Based on Mandala Sand Painting and Group Dialogue.

Outstanding Assignment Award · 2026

Interactive experience

A World in a Grain of Sand

Open full page ↗
#Art X Psychology #Art Therapy #Culturally Adapted #Cultural Psychology

Sand mandala painting is one of the most iconic ceremonial arts in Tibetan Buddhism. Its creation process—constructed outward from the center and ultimately returned to flowing water—embodies the Buddhist concept of impermanence. In recent years, art therapy has increasingly been recognized as a vital form of complementary psychological support; however, how to integrate local cultural resources to develop culturally sensitive art therapy models remains an area requiring further exploration.

Against this backdrop, this proposal attempts to integrate sand mandala painting, art therapy, the Expressive Therapies Continuum (ETC) theoretical framework, and group therapy approaches to propose a participatory art therapy intervention, using sand mandala painting as its primary visual medium.

This design posits that sand mandalas serve not only as a visual vehicle for emotional expression and symbolic construction, but also offer an art therapy practice path for exploring impermanence, accepting change, and building psychological resilience. By transforming traditional cultural resources into viable assets for contemporary psychological support, this intervention offers fresh perspectives on the localization and cross-disciplinary integration of art therapy.

Urban Planning Design

Green Heart Integration & Industrial Heritage

Urban Renewal and Redesign Description of Sinopec Southwest Oilfield Service Company Huayuan Site

#Adaptive Reuse#Industrial Heritage#Inclusive Design#NSGA-II
I

Design Positioning

Grounded in industrial heritage, tied together by an ecological green heart, and guided by community co-creation and sharing, the project aims to create a demonstration area for urban renewal and adaptive reuse that integrates industrial culture exhibition, ecological leisure experience, community living services, and innovative development.

II

Issue Identification

Based on the site analysis, the primary challenges facing the site are as follows:

  • Industrial Heritage UtilizationRich in industrial heritage resources, but with low utilization efficiency, leaving most areas idle.
  • Public Realm & Social InteractionPublic spaces lack vitality, and community social venues are insufficient to meet the needs of diverse demographics.
  • Ecological ConnectivityEcological spaces are fragmented with poor green space continuity.
  • Service InfrastructureService infrastructure is aging, making it difficult to satisfy residents' needs.
III

Design Objectives & Strategies

01

Preserve Industrial Memory and Achieve Sustainable Renewal

Retain historical industrial buildings and spatial site contexts. By integrating new functions and creating immersive scenes, establish an industrial heritage exhibition zone. Introduce creative culture industries and specialty markets to build a sustainable development model that balances conservation, social benefit, and economic efficiency.

02

Reshape the Ecological Framework and Enhance “Green Heart” Functions

Leveraging the ecological green heart resources of the Chang-Zhu-Tan region, use ecological corridors, water systems, and public green spaces as the backbone to form a continuous, open green space system, integrating scattered green pockets into a cohesive ecological network.

03

Activate Community Vitality and Optimize the Service System

Addressing the needs of an aging community and diverse user groups, supplement public service facilities. Construct community plazas, heritage display plazas, senior-friendly plazas, and shared community gardens to create inclusive public spaces for all ages.

IV

Zoning Design via Multi-Objective NSGA-II Optimization

Zone 01

Green Ecological Zone

Integrating the site's ecological foundation and resources, the revitalization strategy for the Green Ecological Zone is defined as:

  • Preserve existing water bodies within the neighborhood and renovate their surrounding environments to create internal pocket parks that provide leisure green space.
  • Unify the planning and management of shared community gardens to meet residents' urban farming needs.
  • Establish a central transitional green buffer strip to minimize the impact of adaptive heritage reuse on residential areas, aligning with the broader regional green heart planning.
02

Heritage Conservation & Adaptive Reuse Zone

Considering building conditions, historical value, and surrounding amenities, the renovation strategy for buildings and public spaces is defined as:

  • Well-preserved industrial buildings with prominent historical value are preserved as protected heritage structures and integrated into an exhibition system showcasing the trajectory of the petroleum industry, creating iconic industrial culture landmarks.
  • Buildings closer to the residential community are repurposed with multifunctional public services—such as cultural markets and community amenities—to generate adaptive value and satisfy residents' needs.
  • Idle factory workshops are injected with commercial formats such as specialty catering, retail, and creative studios. Combined with open public event spaces, this promotes the integrated development of cultural preservation and creative innovation.
06 · Empirical ResearchUrban Health

Research Project, Ma Lab

In this case study, I investigated how commuting attitudes relate to residents' subjective health and life satisfaction using a survey dataset of 3,259 residents in Beijing.

#Commuting#Subjective Health#Life Satisfaction#Beijing

Evidence from Beijing

Main Findings

From 3,259 Beijing residents

01
Commute Duration

Long commuting times have a consistently significant negative impact on both life satisfaction and self-rated health.

02
Preferences for Cars & Shared Mobility

Preferences for private cars, shared transport, walking, and autonomous driving are positively associated with higher life satisfaction and better self-rated health.

03
The Active Commuting Paradox
  • Walking is the only active commuting mode that shows clear benefits for health and well-being.
  • Cycling and e-biking do not show the health benefits typically reported in Western literature; a preference for cycling or e-biking is associated with lower life satisfaction. This suggests that the benefits of active commuting are highly context-dependent, including Beijing's urban density, traffic environment, and socioeconomic factors.

Policy implications

Urban planners should prioritize reducing overall commute times and improving pedestrian-friendly environments, rather than assuming all active travel modes automatically yield health benefits in megacities.

07 · Collaborative ProposalVR Tourism

From Digital Imagination to Physical Exploration: Investigating the Impact of Virtual Tourism on On-Site Visit Intentions in Heritage Districts based on the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)

Historical and cultural heritage sites often face an experiential boundary: while physical architectures remain visible, their underlying historical contexts and cultural narratives are difficult for modern tourists to directly perceive. Addressing this challenge, this proposal investigates how Virtual Reality (VR) tourism experiences can be effectively transformed into physical visitation intentions, taking the Maritime Silk Road Heritage District of Quanzhou Ancient City as a representative case study.

#Virtual Tourism#ELM#Eye Tracking#Spatial CNN

Integrating the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) and Mental Imagery/Imagination Theory, this study constructs a dual-pathway mechanism (“Central Cognitive Path” vs. “Peripheral Affective Path”) to decode the “black box” of tourist decision-making.

Mixed-method approach

A Bio-Psycho-Spatial Methodology

To achieve a holistic “Bio-Psycho-Spatial” methodology, the project combines three complementary dimensions:

  1. 01
    Psychological Dimension

    Subjective Likert-scale surveys measuring central cues (information richness, authenticity, perceived value) and peripheral cues (immersion, aesthetics, entertainment).

  2. 02
    Physiological Dimension

    Eye-tracking experiments measuring real-time, involuntary visual attention and emotional arousal, including gaze duration, fixations, and pupil dilation toward key heritage landmarks.

  3. 03
    Spatial Dimension

    Extending laboratory findings using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) to analyze spatial visual features, enabling automated identification and prediction of urban vitality hotspots across heritage districts.

Research value

Key Expected Contributions

  • Theoretical

    Expands the application of ELM to immersive virtual heritage environments and elucidates how digital imagination drives physical behaviors.

  • Methodological

    Bridges subjective psychology, physiological eye-tracking, and deep-learning spatial algorithms to scale micro-level perception into macro-level spatial insights.

  • Practical

    Offers actionable design and marketing insights for smart heritage management, VR exhibition optimization, and cross-city spatial vitality prediction.

01 · ArcGIS Spatial Analysis

Revealing Injustice in Auckland’s Public Transport Services and Strategies for Improvement—Based on ArcGIS Spatial Analysis

#Spatial Justice#Public Transport#ArcGIS#Auckland
01
Research Background
Demographic & Economic Growth

Auckland is New Zealand’s largest city and economic hub, accommodating over 32% of the national population. Driven by strong overseas migration, rapid population growth has boosted the labor force and GDP while simultaneously expanding the urban footprint.

Severe Traffic Congestion

As an auto-dependent city, Auckland faces persistent, long-standing traffic congestion. Despite major local investments, including the Regional Land Transport Plan, and policy initiatives, severe commuting bottlenecks remain unresolved.

Spatial Transit Equity Concerns

Academic literature indicates that Auckland’s historical transport planning shows heavy path dependence toward road expansion over sustainable transit. Public transport development has also disproportionately favored wealthier neighborhoods.

Research Core Objective

To use ArcGIS spatial analytical techniques—spatial statistics, spatial autocorrelation, hotspot analysis, proximity analysis, overlay analysis, and impedance cost surface modeling—to uncover infrastructure inequities across age and income groups and propose spatial optimization strategies.

02
Analytical Findings

Finding 01

Demographic Inequity & Spatial Clustering
  • Age: Global Moran’s I analysis shows statistically significant positive spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.14, p < 0.01), indicating strong demographic clustering. Getis-Ord Gi hotspot analysis reveals significant youth cold spots in mainland urban areas and a significant aging hotspot on Aotea / Great Barrier Island.
  • Income: Income displays marginal spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.04, p = 0.066). Hotspots cluster around the CBD and immediate high-value corridors, while inner-ring suburban fringes show significantly lower median personal incomes.

Finding 02

Transit Infrastructure Distribution Disparities
  • Network Concentricity: Public transport services—bus, rail, and cycle networks—are heavily skewed toward the CBD and central-to-southern corridors.
  • Structural Mismatches: Thiessen polygon proximity modeling and spatial overlays demonstrate that outer suburban and northwestern areas face severe service coverage gaps. High-demand residential belts in Franklin and Albert-Eden exhibit sparse bus-stop density.

Finding 03

Impedance Cost & Socioeconomic Disconnectivity
  • Minimum cost-distance modeling reveals that central high-income hotspots enjoy low impedance and seamless accessibility, while peripheral lower-income communities such as Māngere-Ōtāhuhu face significantly higher transit impedance despite greater potential transit dependency.
  • The aging population on Aotea / Great Barrier Island is completely deprived of land-based public transit infrastructure.
03
Policy & Strategic Implications
  1. 01
    Target Peripheral Connectivity & Equity

    Expand investment beyond the CBD to improve feeder routes and accessibility in lower-income inner-ring suburbs such as Māngere-Ōtāhuhu, reducing daily commuting costs and impedance for vulnerable groups.

  2. 02
    Priority Expansion to Northern Townships

    Prioritize high-frequency bus and express infrastructure for northern peripheral townships to meet regional demand and reduce highway congestion.

  3. 03
    Demographic-Specific Mobility Solutions

    Introduce targeted, safe, accessible, and age-friendly services for the aging population on Aotea / Great Barrier Island.

  4. 04
    Holistic Policy Alignment

    Integrate physical network optimization with fare-affordability policies so infrastructure availability translates into real-world access and public transit uptake.

02 · Social Neuroscience Review

Decoding Human Social Interaction: Research Hotspots and Future Challenges in fNIRS Hyperscanning

#fNIRS#Hyperscanning#Interbrain Synchrony#Two-Person Neuroscience
01
Research Background
Shift to Two-Person Neuroscience

Traditional cognitive neuroscience treats social interaction as a static stimulus-response process. Two-Person Neuroscience and hyperscanning instead record multiple interacting brains simultaneously to quantify Interbrain Synchrony (IBS) or Interpersonal Neural Synchronization (INS).

Rise of fNIRS Hyperscanning

Compared with rigid, noisy fMRI and motion-sensitive EEG, functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy offers a portable, non-invasive, and motion-tolerant balance that improves ecological validity while retaining adequate spatial localization.

Exponential Literature Growth

Over the past two decades, hyperscanning publications have grown at an annual rate of 27.6%, reflecting a broader movement toward understanding the neural basis of real-world social behavior.

02
Main Analytical Findings & Research Hotspots

The literature consistently highlights pivotal roles for the Prefrontal Cortex (PFC) and Right Temporoparietal Junction (rTPJ).

01
Social Decision-Making

PFC lateralization: Cooperative tasks such as economic games elicit robust IBS in the DLPFC and left PFC, associated with shared cognitive control and strategic decision-making. Competitive tasks rely more heavily on the right PFC.

02
Social Communication & Action Coordination

Mirror neuron and sensorimotor systems: In action imitation and musical duets, followers show heightened temporoparietal and sensorimotor activation to process dynamic social cues. Direct eye contact and close proximity also produce significantly greater PFC synchronization.

03
Close Relationships & Role Dynamics

Teacher-student dyads show functional differentiation and stronger rTPJ–PFC connectivity during interactive exercises. Romantic couples and parent-child dyads show robust INS across frontal, temporal, and parietal networks during joint play and communication, offering neurobiological support for attachment theory.

04
Complex Social Relations

In asymmetric trust games, lower-status investors develop trust toward higher-status trustees more rapidly through an rTPJ–rDLPFC circuit: stronger rTPJ synchrony supports mentalizing while reduced rDLPFC activation lowers cognitive defense and control.

03
Strategic Implications & Future Directions
  1. 01
    Open Science & Data Sharing

    Expand open-access fNIRS hyperscanning repositories—including behavioral annotations and psychological metadata—to accelerate methodological standardization.

  2. 02
    From Correlation to Causality

    Complement correlational IBS measures with Multi-Brain Stimulation technologies, including dual-brain transcranial electrical stimulation, to test direct causal links between synchrony and social behavior.

  3. 03
    Clinical & Underrepresented Populations

    Expand beyond healthy young adults to include people with Autism Spectrum Disorder, clinical populations, and aging demographics.

  4. 04
    Macro-Societal Contexts

    Apply fNIRS hyperscanning to real-world caregiving, patient-doctor, and counseling relationships to improve societal and clinical relevance.

03 · Digital Psychology Review

Fear of Missing Out (FoMO): Unveiling the Links Between Social Media Use, Negative Emotions, and Maladaptive Behaviors

#FoMO#Social Media#Negative Affect#Digital Well-being
01
Research Background
Conceptual Definition

Fear of Missing Out is a pervasive apprehension that others may be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent. Coined by Patrick McGinnis in 2004, it combines regret, fear, and diffuse social anxiety with a desire to remain continually connected.

The Dual Nature of Social Media

Social media lowers barriers to connection and can fulfill belongingness needs, yet its always-on nature amplifies the dread of missing important information or social experiences.

Theoretical Frameworks
  1. Self-Determination Theory: FoMO emerges when basic psychological needs—especially belongingness—are unmet, leading to negative affect and poor self-regulation.
  2. Personality Psychology: FoMO correlates with anxious or avoidant attachment, rumination, narcissism, and low self-esteem, acting as a crucial cognitive and trait variable.
  3. I-PACE Model: Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution factors explain how FoMO mediates between internal states and problematic internet use.
02
Main Analytical Findings
01
Measurement Evolution & Trait-State Distinction

Measurement evolved from Przybylski et al.’s foundational unifactorial scale to multidimensional models such as Wegmann et al.’s distinction between trait-FoMO and online, context-specific state-FoMO. Localized Chinese adaptations have validated these constructs in ecosystems including WeChat and QQ.

02
Ubiquity & Demographic Characteristics

FoMO is validated across Western and Eastern populations. Younger people aged 12–23 show significantly higher levels. Gender findings remain mixed, suggesting different psychological pathways such as rumination, depression, and anxiety.

03
Negative Emotions & Psychopathology

FoMO is strongly associated with anxiety, depression, sadness, and rumination. Neuroimaging evidence suggests that social-media distraction reduces precuneus activity and suppresses high-level visual networks, with high FoMO amplifying cognitive-attentional impairment.

04
Predictor of Maladaptive Social Media Use

FoMO robustly predicts excessive social-media use, smartphone and SNS addiction, impaired self-control, and inappropriate use during lectures or driving.

05
Buffering Mechanisms & Social Support

Authentic self-presentation and positive engagement can occasionally mitigate negative outcomes. Most importantly, perceived social support weakens the path from unmet belongingness needs to FoMO-driven maladaptive behaviors.

03
Strategic Implications & Future Directions
  1. 01
    Conduct Longitudinal Studies

    Move beyond cross-sectional designs to establish directional and causal relationships between FoMO, negative affect, and maladaptive behavior.

  2. 02
    Employ Experimental Methodologies

    Combine self-report surveys with controlled laboratory experiments to reduce social-desirability bias and evaluate behavior objectively.

  3. 03
    Investigate Neural Mechanisms

    Expand neurobiological and neuroimaging research to identify the brain networks and cognitive-control processes involved in FoMO.

  4. 04
    Cross-Cultural Comparisons & Interventions

    Examine how individualism and collectivism shape FoMO while designing interventions that strengthen offline perceived social support.

04 · Comparative Urban Systems Review

Characteristics and Evolution of India’s Urban System: A Literature Review and Implications for China

#Urban Systems#India#China#Comparative Urbanization#Literature Review
01
Research Background
Theoretical Context of Urban Systems

An urban system functions as a nested, multi-tiered spatial structure shaped by population mobility, economic linkages, and spatial interactions across cities of varying scales and functions. Understanding these systems is essential for analyzing national urbanization paths and regional spatial dynamics.

The Indian Context

As one of the world’s most populous developing nations, India is experiencing steady economic growth, economic reforms through LPG policies, and rapid expansion of Special Economic Zones. Yet its urbanization path exhibits structural imbalances that diverge from standard development models.

Research Gap & Purpose

Existing scholarship examines individual megacities and regional disparities, but a comprehensive comparative synthesis remains valuable. This review examines the structural characteristics, spatial patterns, and future trends of India’s urban system, then compares them with China’s urbanization path to identify strategic policy insights.

02
Analytical Findings & Results

Finding 01

Low National Primacy, Increasingly Top-Heavy Hierarchy
  • Conformance to Zipf’s Law: Unlike many developing nations dominated by a single megacity, India’s national city-size distribution generally follows Zipf’s Law. Its colonial history and federal political system distributed power across state-level regional hubs.
  • Top-Heavy Urban Structure: Class-I cities with populations above 100,000 accommodated roughly 26% of India’s urban population in 1901; by 2011, their share had risen above 65% while the share in smaller towns steadily declined.

Finding 02

High Concentration at the State Level

Despite relatively low primacy at the national scale, strong spatial concentration is widespread at the sub-national level. Primary state capitals and large regional centers dominate their respective hinterlands, producing uneven state-level urban hierarchies.

Finding 03

Spatial Disparities, Mobility Constraints & Social Costs
  • Stable Core Growth Poles: Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai remain central national nodes, alongside emerging high-tech centers such as Bengaluru and Hyderabad.
  • North–South and Coastal–Inland Divergence: Southern and coastal states show higher urbanization, denser urban populations, and stronger links to global high-tech value chains than northern inland regions.
  • Expansion Outpaces Population Growth: Rapid land expansion has produced dispersed sprawl and landscape fragmentation in major cities.
  • Institutional Constraints: Caste-linked social structures restrict cross-regional labor migration, keeping much population movement short-distance or rural-to-rural. Infrastructure shortfalls also contribute to persistent slum populations.

Finding 04

SEZs, Urban Corridors & Megalopolises
  • SEZ-Driven Agglomeration: Special Economic Zones and IT-driven services have transformed major cities into critical nodes of global capital.
  • Emergence of Urban Corridors: Improved transport links are connecting primary nodes into corridors and five key mega-regions, including Mumbai–Pune, Delhi–NCR, and Bengaluru–Mysore. Urban clusters are becoming the primary scale of future economic growth.
03
Implications & Strategic Insights for China
  1. 01
    Institutional Drivers of Urban Structure

    China’s Hukou system and India’s caste dynamics and federal system demonstrate how institutional arrangements shape population mobility and the structure of national city systems.

  2. 02
    The Limits of Agglomeration without Integration

    Growth poles and market-driven agglomeration without adequate infrastructure, social integration, or equitable public services can produce persistent slums and severe regional inequality.

  3. 03
    Validating New-Type Urbanization

    India’s challenges reinforce the value of China’s New-Type Urbanization (新型城镇化) and county-level economic development (县域经济). Strengthening small-to-medium cities and county hubs can prevent excessive megacity concentration, support smoother labor migration, and foster a resilient, multi-tiered urban hierarchy.

01 · Poetry & Humanistic Criticism

Beyond Description: A Multidimensional Reading of Elizabeth Bishop’s The Armadillo

#Elizabeth Bishop#Close Reading#Anti-War Poetics#Religion#Humanistic Ethics

Elizabeth Bishop’s poetry has long been known for its calm, restrained, and precise style of description. She avoids direct self-exposure and political stance; instead, she tends to show her ideas indirectly through careful observation of natural scenes, concrete objects, and everyday experience. “Emotion is essential,” Bishop once said in an interview, emphasizing that readers should not overlook the lyrical composition of her work.

The Armadillo is one of her most famous poems. It combines delicate observation with lyrical technique, and scholars have discussed its possible meanings from many angles. This essay brings those interpretive perspectives together to understand an implicit yet powerful poem through a comprehensive lens.

I
Delicate Description: The Shift from Narrative to Metaphor

The opening of The Armadillo is strongly concrete and narrative. The poem first describes the festival and the fire balloons rising slowly into the night sky:

This is the time of year
when almost every night
the frail, illegal fire balloons appear.
Climbing the mountain height,

rising toward a saint
still honored in these parts,
the paper chambers flush and fill with light
that comes and goes, like hearts.

The balloons move gently upward, creating a beautiful and sacred scene. Bishop uses words such as “frail” and “illegal” to describe the fire balloons while quietly planting hints of what will come later. Rather than offering immediate judgment, she speaks as a narrator and allows readers to immerse themselves in the beauty of the festival.

However, tension slowly accumulates within this narrative description:

or the pale green one. With a wind,
they flare and falter, wobble and toss;
but if it’s still they steer between
the kite sticks of the Southern Cross,

receding, dwindling, solemnly
and steadily forsaking us,
or, in the downdraft from a peak,
suddenly turning dangerous.

The balloons shake and bump, creating a lively sense of motion. Later, a balloon falls “like an egg,” recalling its earlier fragility, and causes the fire to spread. Bishop transforms firelight that once resembled stars into a dangerous blaze, creating a dynamic and lyrical prose style.

Scholars have noted that the sculptor Alexander Calder influenced Bishop’s poetic dynamics: lyric becomes a system coordinating the drift, shift, and weight of the external world with inner thought. In The Armadillo, fire balloons rise, fall, float, and drop, and their movement matches the poem’s spiritual and emotional changes.

II
Religious Features: Questioning the Call of Faith

Once up against the sky it’s hard
to tell them from the stars—
planets, that is—the tinted ones:
Venus going down, or Mars,

In Brazil, June 24 marks the Festa de São João, a nationwide folk festival with deep religious roots. Bishop uses several terms associated with Christianity, including “the Southern Cross” and “solemnly.” When the poem blurs the distinction between fire balloons and stars, it becomes possible to read these human-made objects as a disruption of celestial order.

Line 38, “O falling fire and piercing cry,” begins with “O,” an exclamation often used in Christian invocation. Combined with the catastrophe that follows, these religious signs suggest that Bishop may be questioning the call of faith. Although the festival balloons traditionally symbolize light and blessing, their use also reveals a darker side: they aestheticize danger and displace responsibility.

III
Anti-War Poetics: Festival Fire as a Metaphor for Modern Violence

The Armadillo is often situated within the Cold War and Vietnam War context. Scholars have read both The Armadillo and In the Waiting Room as possessing a complex anti-war consciousness. From this perspective, the fire and its victims—the owls, the armadillo, and the rabbit—are translated into political imagery.

The fire balloon is first compared with the human heart—fragile and full of light—but suddenly becomes dangerous. This shift is not only a description of an actual event; researchers argue that it is also imbued with anti-war consciousness.

Why, then, is the poem titled The Armadillo rather than The Owls or The Rabbit? The answer may lie in the animal’s image:

The ancient owls’ nest must have burned.
Hastily, all alone,
a glistening armadillo left the scene,
rose-flecked, head down, tail down,

The armadillo’s shell, and its ability to curl into a ball for protection, echo the final line: “a weak mailed fist clenched ignorant against the sky!” It represents the helpless protest of the innocent. The historical image of a female warrior riding an armadillo also personified America in European eyes, presenting the continent as a backward “Other” and as a land conquered without mercy. The armadillo therefore becomes more than a victim of a festival accident; it condenses the experience of historical and political victims.

Others regard this political reading as excessive because the poem influenced Robert Lowell, whose work expressed an overt anti-war position. Such criticism asks readers to set aside Lowell’s influence and return to Bishop’s own experience: the fire threatened her home and the life of her local community. Either way, the falling fire leads the poet to reflect on what an “ignorance of consequences” can produce and how disastrous those results may be.

IV
Human Concern: Lyric Feeling and Ethics

Many of Bishop’s poems depict animals, including The Fish, Sandpiper, and Pink Dog. Although it is plausible to view Bishop as an environmental poet, scholars have argued that she uses animal figures and ecological criticism to mediate between humanity and nature. In The Armadillo, she transfers moral anger onto suffering animals, a move more powerful than direct human speech.

Through delicate language and concrete imagery, Bishop communicates deeper meanings behind the scene. She asks readers to look into a two-sided circumstance: the fire balloon supports local values and faith, yet it also destroys ordinary life. She leads readers to recognize the coexistence of beauty and danger and the risks of ignorance. Rather than being simply an environmentalist, Bishop emerges as a writer with an awakened social consciousness.

V
Conclusion

The Armadillo is a poem of complex and profound meaning, inviting interpretation from multiple perspectives. It exposes precarious relationships among faith, humanity, nature, and society, all of which underscore Bishop’s deep humanistic consciousness.

The armadillo disrupts the scenic beauty established at the poem’s opening. This aligns with Slavoj Žižek’s suggestion that the “Other” is not a contained element within a stable system, but a “crack” revealing the inner instability and contradictions of that system’s meaning. In Bishop’s work, the suffering animal becomes that crack, shattering the aesthetic surface of a dangerous reality.

Ultimately, the charm of Bishop’s poetry lies in her refusal to adopt a loud political stance. Instead, she guides readers to reflect on the coexistence of beauty and danger, urging us to recognize the aftermath of an “ignorance of consequences.”

02 · Cross-Cultural Art History

Flowers in Bloom: Expressions of Self-Consciousness in 17th-Century Chinese and Dutch Female Floral Still-Life Painters

#Women Artists#Floral Still-Life#China & the Netherlands#Self-Consciousness#Visual Culture
01
Research Background
Historical Marginalization

Female artists have historically been relegated to the margins of patriarchal art narratives. Despite institutional and social restrictions, women played an undeniable role in art history.

Parallel 17th-Century Developments

China underwent the Ming–Qing transition while the Dutch Republic rose as a global maritime power. Across these distinct contexts, increasing numbers of female painters turned to floral still-life as an important medium.

Why Flowers?

In China, restrictions on women’s movement made domestic flora accessible subjects, while orchids and bamboo carried metaphors of moral character, feminine beauty, and impermanence. In the Dutch Republic, exclusion from life drawing and anatomy courses made still-life a viable professional path amid merchant demand and scientific interest in botany.

Research Gap & Purpose

Moving beyond individual case studies and regional overviews, this cross-cultural comparison examines how Chinese and Dutch women used floral still-life to express self-consciousness within patriarchal structures.

02
Four Artists, Four Forms of Agency

The comparison crosses social class, geography, artistic convention, and professional opportunity.

CN · 01

Courtesan Painter

Ma Shouzhen / Ma Xianglan

Social context: Ming-dynasty courtesans used art to engage male literati, adopting wenren aesthetics to gain social status and cultural acceptance.

Self-expression: Ma specialized in “unrooted orchids,” symbols of pure moral character and of her own transient, homeless existence. The motif appealed to literati taste while giving form to her emotions and personal identity.

CN · 02

Gentry / Boudoir Painter

Chen Shu

Social context: Chen worked within Confucian roles as a virtuous wife and mother. Her eldest son presented her paintings to the Qing court, making her one of the best-represented female artists in the imperial collection.

Self-expression: Works such as New Year’s Display combined imitation of earlier masters with delicate personal brushwork and auspicious symbols of longevity and peace. Mastery of conservative norms became a way to secure recognition and carve out artistic agency.

NL · 03

Botany & Scientific Illustration

Maria Sibylla Merian

Social context: Colonial expansion encouraged demand for studies of exotic flora and fauna. Excluded from formal guilds in Frankfurt and Nuremberg, Merian used family ties in art to pursue independent scientific work and travel.

Self-expression: Her journey to Dutch Surinam enabled close documentation of native insects and plants. By uniting artistic skill with rigorous empirical observation, she pioneered scientific insect-and-plant illustration.

NL · 04

Symbolism & Vanitas

Rachel Ruysch

Social context: Ruysch became one of the most celebrated Dutch artists, the first female member of The Hague painters’ guild, and a court painter.

Self-expression: Her asymmetric bouquets, dramatic chiaroscuro, out-of-season flowers, and wilting petals embedded vanitas reflections on life’s transience. Subtle self-portraits reflected on glossy surfaces served as signatures of artistic self-awareness.

03
Implications & Strategic Insights
  1. 01
    Negotiating Patriarchal Boundaries

    Chinese and Dutch women worked within gendered spaces allowed by patriarchal societies, yet transformed those constraints into platforms for artistic agency and professional distinction.

  2. 02
    The Dual Function of Flowers

    Floral painting conformed to literati taste, market demand, and scientific realism while also mirroring personal emotion, philosophical reflection, and self-identity.

  3. 03
    Why Cross-Cultural Art History Matters

    Comparing Eastern and Western painters reveals shared patterns in how marginalized artists navigate social boundaries, repositioning women as active contributors rather than decorative footnotes in art history.

03 · Literature, Philosophy & Spatial Design

The Pinnacle of Landscape Garden Art in the Wei–Jin Period: Xie Lingyun’s Garden Aesthetic Philosophy Through Shanju Fu

#Xie Lingyun#Chinese Gardens#Shanju Fu#Spatial Aesthetics#Buddhism & Daoism
01
Research Background
A Paradigm Shift in Garden History

The Wei, Jin, and Northern and Southern Dynasties marked a transition from the formation of Chinese garden culture toward an early peak. Political instability and the convergence of Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism, and Neo-Daoist xuanxue encouraged literati to seek natural hermitages. Gardens became spiritual sanctuaries and conscious artistic creations rather than passive imitations of nature.

Xie Lingyun and Landscape Poetry

As a foundational figure in Chinese landscape poetry, Xie Lingyun (385–433) sought solace in nature and religious contemplation after political disillusionment. Shanju Fu records both his spatial design of the Shining Estate (始宁庄园) and his philosophical reflection upon it.

Research Gap & Purpose

Literary scholarship often criticized the work’s elaborate Han-rhapsody form, while garden historians focused mainly on its descriptions. This study bridges literary analysis and spatial aesthetics to show how Xie combined Han literary forms with Buddhist and Daoist principles to forge an early landmark in Chinese garden aesthetics.

02
Four Principles of Garden Aesthetics
01周圆之美

Enclosing Harmony

Panoramic and Multi-Dimensional Spatial Perception

Xie inherited the expansive Han fu style to organize the estate across four cardinal directions and two depth levels, near and far. Unlike a fixed single-point perspective, his shifting “panoramic flyover” gaze creates a multi-focal, layered composition in which spatial rhythm and depth become an inner mental landscape.

02动静不二

Non-Duality of Motion and Stillness

Zen Rhythms in Water Phenomena

The immense estate incorporated water in many forms, from rushing waterfalls to quiet streams beneath rocks. Their interplay embodies the Chan/Zen principle of finding absolute quietude within movement and active vitality within stillness, probing the origin of life itself.

03无饰之饰

Unadorned Decoration

Symbiosis of Architecture and Nature

Structures such as the Tongting Pavilion and West Pavilion were sited to capture river currents, mountain peaks, and misty horizons. Instead of artificial ornament, red clouds, green foliage, seasonal change, and shifting light decorated the architecture. Reciprocal viewing anticipated techniques of framed and borrowed scenery.

04曲径通幽

Winding Paths Leading to Seclusion

Adaptive Paths and Spiritual Retreat

Winding routes between the northern and southern residences followed natural terrain through bamboo groves, mountain ridges, and steep gorges. Their continually shifting views produced profound seclusion, physically manifesting Buddhist and Daoist ideals of hermit life.

03
Implications & Broader Significance
  1. 01
    An Early Theoretical Landmark

    Predating Ji Cheng’s Yuanye (The Craft of Gardens) by more than a millennium, Shanju Fu fuses spatial practice with aesthetic theory in the design of a large private estate.

  2. 02
    Literature, Philosophy & Space

    Xie’s elaborate literary technique was tied to his aesthetic consciousness, bridging actual landscape design with spiritual enlightenment and moving landscape art from realistic description toward spiritual resonance and intuitive realization.

  3. 03
    A Cornerstone of Classical Garden Aesthetics

    Borrowed scenery, multi-focal spatial enclosure, seasonal adaptability, and naturalistic integration laid essential ideological and aesthetic foundations for classical Chinese garden design across later dynasties.
